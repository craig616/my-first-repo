# Writing style: Nigel Slater-inspired

Inspired by his voice, never copying his text. Short quoted fragments below are for reference only and must not be reused in our notes.

## The voice in one line
A friend cooking alongside you: unhurried, practical, quietly pleased with good simple food, and trusting you to taste and decide.

## What we observed (20 BBC recipes)

**1. Loose measures, kept honest.** Everyday measures sit next to exact ones: "a good handful", "a few glugs", "a thick slice of butter", "2 good spilling handfuls of arborio rice (about 200g)".
→ For us: you *may* use a loose phrase, but the exact amount always follows in brackets. Cook mode needs the number.

**2. Sizes from everyday objects.** "Pieces the shape of a postage stamp", rolled out to the thickness of a £1 coin, "a wineglass of Madeira".
→ Use one such reference where it really helps with size or thickness. Never more than one per recipe.

**3. Time paired with a sensory cue.** "Fry for 4–6 minutes until golden-brown"; leeks "translucent but not coloured"; salmon done when "the flakes part easily"; risotto "plump and creamy but still has a little bite".
→ Every timed step gets a cue for what you'll see, smell or feel. The cue matters more than the clock.

**4. The reason, in brackets.** Asides that explain *why*: the Parmesan "will melt and help to hold the fritter together"; work quickly because the bicarbonate "will start working immediately"; soda bread "is best eaten warm".
→ At most one short bracketed reason per step, and only when it helps the cook.

**5. Permission and flexibility.** "As much coriander as you like", "feel free to vary the ingredients", "if you like"; taste the apples for sweetness and add sugar "accordingly"; rub the butter in by hand if you don't have a food processor.
→ Offer the swap or the no-gadget route. Invite tasting.

**6. Small rules of thumb.** Don't play with the fritters; let a crust form before turning. Turn meatballs "no more than once or twice" so they become crisp and sticky.
→ Pass on the one technique tip that makes the difference.

**7. Sparing first person.** "I like mine still pink and a little springy in the middle." "I tend to turn them…"
→ "I" no more than once or twice a recipe, and only for a preference, never for a fact.

**8. Tactile, plain verbs.** rip, tear, tip, scatter, tuck, nestle, trickle, crumble, a dollop, a glug, spike (a cake with a skewer).
→ Prefer these to "add", "combine" or "incorporate".

**9. Endings that land.** "Eat immediately, while all is hot, herbal and piquant." "Serve straightaway scattered with more coriander." "Awfully good with a little chutney."
→ The last step says how to eat it, with one note of feeling or a suggestion for what to serve alongside.

**10. Intros are short and give a reason to cook.** One or two sentences: the pleasure (crunch, "very tasty indeed"), the occasion (Boxing Day, a quick lunch, coming home), or the thrift (leftovers, little washing up, fruit past its best: "far too good for the compost heap"). Praise is understated: "awfully good", "a real treat".

**11. Gentle wit, never jokes.** "Just eat the bowl when you've finished." A dry line, at most once.

**12. Thrift and the seasons.** Leftovers, the stock from a Sunday roast, fruit past its best, what's in season. Mention these when they're true of the recipe.

## Rules for our notes
- **Intro:** 2–3 sentences. The reason to cook it plus the occasion or the thrift. Understated, with no exclamation marks.
- **Steps:** one action per step, 1–3 sentences. Exact quantities, temperatures (`200°C / 180°C fan / gas 6`) and times, each with a sensory cue. Use tactile verbs. At most one bracketed reason.
- **Last step:** how to serve and eat it.
- **Notes:** swaps, the no-gadget route, what to serve with it, how it keeps.
- **Never:** hype ("game-changer", "hack", "insane", "the best ever"), exclamation marks, invented facts about the creator, or changing quantities for the sake of the voice.
